\section{Deliberation on Task 1}
\label{sec:part2-deliberation}

This section reflects on the ethics and potential bias of the Individual Task~1
analysis. In Task~1, XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) were trained separately
on two banking datasets: Portuguese Bank Marketing for term-deposit subscription and
Credit Card Default for prediction of default payment next month. The deliberation
below examines whether the methodology supported an unbiased evaluation, how model
performance changes with training-set size, whether subgroup disparities are
observable using Microsoft Fairlearn, and what security, privacy, and ethical risks
could arise if the methodology were deployed at scale or transferred to another
application.

\paragraph{Scope note on Bank Marketing features.}
Unless otherwise stated, all Bank Marketing analyses in this Part~2 deliberation
\emph{exclude} the call-\texttt{duration} feature. Duration is only observed after the
marketing call and therefore constitutes post-call information leakage if used for
pre-call targeting. Task~1 reported a with-duration result as an exploratory upper
bound; Part~2 uses the leakage-free feature set so that learning curves, fairness
audits, and deployment discussion refer to information that would actually be
available when deciding whom to contact.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cross-Validation and Sampling Strategy}
\label{sec:part2-cv}
%----------------------------------------------------------

An unbiased evaluation requires that reported metrics reflect generalisation to
unseen clients rather than accidental performance on a favourable partition. Both
datasets are class-imbalanced, with approximately 11.7\% positive subscriptions in
Bank Marketing and 22.1\% defaults in Credit Card Default. Under such imbalance, a
simple random split can produce unstable minority-class representation and distort
both ranking and classification metrics. Accordingly, Task~1 used a stratified 80/20
train--test holdout so that the positive-class proportions were approximately
preserved across the training and test partitions. Stratification was appropriate
because the minority class was also the operational class of interest: subscribers
for marketing and defaulters for credit-risk analysis.

A limitation of Task~1 is that the original evaluation relied on a single stratified
holdout rather than repeated or $k$-fold cross-validation. Although stratification
reduced variation in minority-class representation, performance could still depend on
the particular random partition. Five-fold stratified cross-validation in Part~2
therefore provides a more robust estimate of generalisation by evaluating the models
across multiple partitions.

Preprocessing was appropriately contained within scikit-learn pipelines, reducing the
risk that transformations learned from evaluation data contaminated model training.
Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, while numeric variables were standardised
for the MLP. However, imbalance was not handled identically across the two algorithms.
XGBoost used \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, whereas the MLP did not receive an
equivalent class-weighting mechanism. Consequently, differences in minority-class
recall may reflect both the underlying model architectures and differences in how
class imbalance was handled.

For Part~2, five-fold stratified cross-validation was also used during learning-curve
estimation. Stratified $k$-fold preserves class proportions across folds and reduces
the probability that a single favourable or unfavourable partition dominates the
conclusion. Average precision (PR-AUC) remained the principal evaluation metric,
consistent with Task~1. For these imbalanced banking tasks, performance on the rare
positive class is more informative than overall accuracy because high accuracy can be
achieved while still failing to identify many subscribers or defaulters.

Synthetic oversampling such as SMOTE was not used as the primary imbalance strategy.
This choice avoids creating synthetic combinations of financial and demographic
attributes that may not correspond to realistic customers. Instead, stratification
preserved the original outcome distribution and XGBoost used class reweighting to
reduce majority-class dominance during optimisation.

A further methodological limitation concerns the exploratory threshold search in
Task~1. Candidate classification thresholds were evaluated using the held-out test
labels to identify the threshold maximising positive-class F1. Although the principal
reported Task~1 results used the default threshold of 0.5, selecting an operational
threshold using test-set labels can introduce optimistic bias. A stronger procedure
would determine thresholds entirely within the training data, for example through
cross-validation, and reserve the final test set for a single unbiased evaluation.

Taken together, stratification, holdout testing, and stratified cross-validation reduce
evaluation bias caused by unstable sampling, but they do not remove bias from the
underlying data-generating process. A statistically reliable evaluation protocol can
still reveal inequitable behaviour if historical labels, sampling processes, or
features encode different outcomes across demographic groups.

\subsection{Learning Curves under Varying Training-Set Sizes}
\label{sec:part2-learning-curves}
%----------------------------------------------------------

To analyse how model performance depends on the amount of labelled data, learning
curves were generated for XGBoost and MLP on both datasets. Training sizes were set to
10\%, 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\% of the available training pool, with five-fold
stratified cross-validation and PR-AUC as the principal metric.
Figures~\ref{fig:part2-bank-lc} and~\ref{fig:part2-credit-lc} show \emph{both}
training and validation PR-AUC so that train--validation gaps can be inspected
directly rather than asserted without evidence.

    versus number of training samples for XGBoost and MLP using five-fold stratified

    \label{fig:part2-credit-lc}
\end{figure}

On Bank Marketing without \texttt{duration}, both models improve as more training data
become available, but absolute validation PR-AUC is substantially lower than the
Task~1 with-duration upper bound. XGBoost validation PR-AUC rises from approximately
0.39 at 3{,}616 samples to approximately 0.46 at full size, while training PR-AUC
falls from approximately 0.90 to 0.57. The train--validation gap therefore narrows from
about 0.51 to 0.11. This pattern is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:part2-bank-lc} and is
consistent with overfitting at small sample sizes followed by improved generalisation
as more labelled campaigns are added.

The MLP begins with lower and more variable performance at small $n$ and reaches a
validation PR-AUC of approximately 0.43 at full size. Its train--validation gap remains
comparatively small (around 0.04--0.06), suggesting less memorisation than XGBoost at
small samples, but also a weaker fit overall. Under the leakage-free feature set,
XGBoost remains ahead of the MLP on validation PR-AUC across all examined data budgets.

On Credit Card Default, absolute PR-AUC values remain moderate. XGBoost validation
PR-AUC improves from roughly 0.51 to 0.56 as training size increases, while training
PR-AUC falls from about 0.98 to 0.68 and the gap narrows from about 0.47 to 0.13
(Figure~\ref{fig:part2-credit-lc}). The MLP improves from a lower base toward
approximately 0.54 and maintains a small train--validation gap. The diminishing
late-stage gains suggest that further identical labelled observations would help only
marginally without richer features or temporal validation.

Overall, the curves support two conclusions. First, XGBoost's advantage over the MLP
under PR-AUC is stable across training-set sizes for both tasks. Second, excluding
\texttt{duration} materially reduces Bank Marketing performance relative to Task~1,
which is expected and methodologically preferable for any discussion of pre-call
deployment.

\subsection{Bias Analysis with Microsoft Fairlearn}
\label{sec:part2-fairness}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Observed subgroup disparities were audited using Microsoft Fairlearn
\cite{bird2020fairlearn}. Fairlearn was chosen because it integrates directly with
scikit-learn-style estimators, supports group-wise \texttt{MetricFrame} reporting, and
provides standard disparity measures including demographic parity difference and
equalized odds difference. Compared with dashboard-centric alternatives such as
Google What-If, Fairlearn is well suited to a scripted and reproducible workflow that
is consistent with the Task~1 training pipeline.

The fairness audit focuses on XGBoost because Task~1 identified it as the stronger
model on minority-sensitive PR-AUC, recall, and F1 metrics across both datasets,
making it the more plausible candidate for downstream use. Two sensitive attributes
were examined: age group for Bank Marketing (\texttt{<30}, \texttt{30--59},
\texttt{60+}) and sex for Credit Card Default (male/female as coded in the dataset).
Age was grouped rather than treated as a continuous variable so that disparities could
be interpreted across meaningful cohorts. The audited Bank Marketing model again
excludes \texttt{duration}. Evaluation used a stratified holdout and a 0.5
classification threshold.

These fairness measures identify differences between groups rather than proving that
the model is discriminatory. Observed disparities can arise from differences already
present in historical outcome rates, from differences introduced or amplified by the
model, or from interactions between the two. Reporting subgroup sample sizes and
observed positive outcome rates is therefore essential: without them, selection-rate
gaps can be misread as pure model bias when they partly reflect base-rate differences
or unstable estimates in small cohorts.

For Bank Marketing, the audit identifies substantial age-related disparity even after
removing \texttt{duration}. The demographic parity difference is approximately 0.57
and the equalized odds difference approximately 0.52. Table~\ref{tab:part2-bank-fair}
shows that the $60+$ group is small ($n=337$) but has both a higher historical
subscription base rate (0.33) and a much higher model selection rate (0.74), together
with a markedly elevated false positive rate (0.64). The modal age group 30--59
($n=7703$) has the lowest base rate (0.10) and the lowest selection rate (0.16).
Clients under 30 ($n=1003$) fall between these extremes.

This pattern cannot be reduced to base-rate differences alone: selection for the $60+$
group (0.74) far exceeds its already higher base rate (0.33), and the false positive
rate indicates that many non-subscribers in that group would still be contacted. In
practice, a campaign optimiser could therefore over-contact older customers while
contacting middle-aged customers less frequently. Because marketing contact is itself
an intervention, and because the $60+$ estimate rests on a comparatively small $n$,
both fairness and statistical uncertainty are ethically relevant even when aggregate
PR-AUC looks acceptable.

For Credit Card Default, sex-related disparities are smaller but still observable
(Table~\ref{tab:part2-credit-fair}). Demographic parity and equalized odds differences
are both approximately 0.057. Male customers ($n=2402$) have a slightly higher base
rate (0.234 vs 0.213) and a higher selection rate (0.316 vs 0.259), with a higher
false positive rate (0.224 vs 0.167) and similar true positive rates. Group sizes are
large enough that the gaps are unlikely to be sampling noise alone. In a credit-risk
context, even moderate false-positive gaps can translate into unequal scrutiny.

An important caveat is that fairness results are threshold-dependent and can also be
affected by whether a sensitive feature is directly included in the predictive model.
Task~1 included age in the Bank Marketing model and sex in the Credit Default model.
A more comprehensive study could therefore compare performance after removing these
attributes, examine possible proxy variables, or apply Fairlearn mitigation techniques
such as constrained optimisation or post-processing. The present analysis is
diagnostic rather than remedial, which is appropriate for reflecting on the original
Task~1 methodology.

\subsection{Security, Privacy, and Ethical Risks at Scale}
\label{sec:part2-risks}
%----------------------------------------------------------

If the Task~1 methodology were deployed widely, for example as a bank-wide marketing
scorer or a credit early-warning system, several additional risks would arise.

\paragraph{(i) Security.}
Model outputs, training data, and feature-processing pipelines would become valuable
attack targets. An adversary with repeated access to a prediction API could attempt
model extraction or membership-inference attacks, particularly if detailed probability
scores were exposed. Customers or intermediaries could also manipulate modifiable
inputs such as contact-channel information, repayment timing, payment amounts, or
other behavioural variables in an attempt to influence model outputs. At larger scale,
training-data poisoning or unauthorised model replacement could systematically affect
predictions for many customers. Appropriate controls would include access-controlled
scoring endpoints, encryption, audit logging, model and data versioning, signed model
artefacts, monitoring for unusual query behaviour, and human review for high-impact
decisions.

\paragraph{(ii) Privacy.}
The datasets contain sensitive or potentially quasi-identifying attributes including
age, job, marital status, education, sex, account behaviour, credit limits, bill
amounts, payment amounts, and repayment history. Large-scale deployment would likely
involve retaining behavioural data for repeated scoring and model retraining, creating
risks of re-identification, unauthorised secondary use, and function creep. For
example, a score originally developed for marketing could later be reused for credit
assessment without customers expecting that use. Privacy-preserving deployment would
therefore require data minimisation, clearly defined purpose limitation, restricted
retention periods, access controls, encryption at rest and in transit, and governance
over reuse of sensitive information.

Historical financial data can also encode previous institutional practices and social
inequalities. Removing direct identifiers does not necessarily prevent sensitive
information from being inferred from combinations of demographic and behavioural
variables. Privacy and fairness therefore need to be considered together rather than
as independent technical requirements.

\paragraph{(iii) Ethical Risks.}
The Fairlearn analysis demonstrates that strong aggregate predictive performance does
not guarantee equitable treatment across groups. A marketing model that substantially
over-selects older customers could contribute to excessive or potentially
manipulative targeting if combined with aggressive sales practices. In credit-risk
settings, false positive predictions may expose individuals to unnecessary scrutiny
or influence downstream decisions concerning financial access.

Transferring the same methodology into a different application could create even
greater ethical risks. For example, using repayment-derived variables for employment
screening or insurance pricing would apply data collected for one purpose to decisions
with fundamentally different consequences. Such transfer would require new validation,
new fairness analysis, and a clear justification of whether the variables are
appropriate for the new context.

Ethical deployment would therefore require explicit purpose constraints, continuous
subgroup monitoring, transparent threshold policies, human oversight for adverse
decisions, meaningful explanations, and mechanisms for affected individuals to contest
or correct decisions. Transparency about uncertainty is also important. Task~1 showed
that call duration substantially increased Bank Marketing performance even though it
is only known after the marketing call; Part~2 therefore excludes duration so that
deployment discussion is not based on an inflated, non-operational feature set.

\subsection{Summary and Reflection}
\label{sec:part2-summary}
%----------------------------------------------------------

The Part~2 deliberation changes how the original Task~1 results should be interpreted.
The stratified 80/20 holdout was a reasonable first evaluation strategy for the
imbalanced datasets, and the use of PR-AUC, positive-class recall, and F1 was more
appropriate than relying on accuracy alone. However, the analysis also had important
limitations: performance was initially assessed using only one train--test partition,
the two models did not receive identical treatment for class imbalance, and exploratory
threshold optimisation used information from the held-out test labels.

Five-fold stratified cross-validation and the learning-curve analysis---now showing
both training and validation curves---provide stronger evidence that XGBoost's
advantage over the MLP is relatively stable across partitions and training-set sizes.
Excluding \texttt{duration} lowers Bank Marketing PR-AUC relative to Task~1 but yields
a more honest estimate for pre-call use. Fairlearn further shows that aggregate
performance does not imply equitable subgroup behaviour: age disparities in Bank
Marketing remain large after removing duration, while Credit Default shows smaller but
visible sex-related differences, now reported with subgroup $n$ and base rates.

If the analysis were repeated, threshold selection would be performed entirely inside
the training or validation process, subgroup fairness metrics would be included as
standard evaluation criteria alongside predictive performance, and deployment-relevant
features would continue to be separated from variables unavailable at decision time.
External or temporal validation would also be required before transferring the models
to a different institution, population, or application.

Overall, the reflection shows that model quality cannot be assessed solely through
PR-AUC or other aggregate predictive metrics. Responsible evaluation must also consider
sampling stability, information leakage, subgroup error distributions, privacy,
security, deployment context, and the potential consequences of model-assisted
decisions.
