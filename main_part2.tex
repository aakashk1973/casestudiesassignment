\section{Part 2: Deliberation on Task 1}
\label{sec:part2-deliberation}

This section reflects on the ethics and potential bias of the Part~1 analysis.
In Part~1, XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) were trained separately on
two banking datasets: Portuguese bank marketing (term-deposit subscription) and
UCI credit-card default prediction. The deliberation below examines whether the
methodology supported an unbiased evaluation, how performance changes with
training-set size, whether subgroup bias is observable under Microsoft Fairlearn,
and what security, privacy, and ethical risks would arise if the approach were
deployed at scale.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cross-Validation and Sampling Strategy}
\label{sec:part2-cv}
%----------------------------------------------------------

An unbiased evaluation requires that the reported metrics reflect generalisation
to unseen clients rather than accidental memorisation of a favourable split.
Both datasets are strongly class-imbalanced (approximately 11.7\% positive
subscriptions and 22.1\% defaults). Under such imbalance, a single random split
that does not preserve class proportions can distort both accuracy-oriented and
ranking metrics. Accordingly, Part~1 used a stratified 80/20 train--test holdout
so that the positive-class rate in the test set matched the overall population
rate. Stratification was preferred to simple random sampling because the minority
class is precisely the class of operational interest: subscribers in marketing
and defaulters in credit risk.

For Part~2, evaluation was strengthened with five-fold stratified cross-validation
inside learning-curve estimation. Stratified $k$-fold keeps class proportions
stable across folds and reduces the chance that a single unlucky partition
dominates the conclusion. The scoring metric remained average precision (PR-AUC),
consistent with Part~1's case-aligned evaluation: in banking applications with
costly false negatives, ranking quality on the rare positive class is more
informative than overall accuracy. Class imbalance was also addressed during
training through XGBoost's \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, which rebalances the
learning objective without fabricating synthetic minority samples. Synthetic
oversampling (e.g.\ SMOTE) was not used as the primary strategy because it can
alter the empirical distribution of banking features in ways that are difficult
to justify operationally; reweighting preserves the original feature space while
still correcting gradient dominance by the majority class.

Taken together, stratification, holdout testing, and stratified cross-validation
do not eliminate bias in the \emph{data-generating process}, but they do reduce
evaluation bias caused by unstable sampling. That distinction matters: a fair
evaluation protocol can still reveal unfair models if the underlying historical
labels encode unequal outcomes across groups.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Learning Curves under Varying Training-Set Sizes}
\label{sec:part2-learning-curves}
%----------------------------------------------------------

To analyse how model performance depends on the amount of labelled data,
learning curves were generated for both models on both datasets. Training sizes
were set to 10\%, 25\%, 50\%, 75\%, and 100\% of the available training pool,
with five-fold stratified cross-validation and PR-AUC as the evaluation metric.
Figures~\ref{fig:part2-bank-lc} and~\ref{fig:part2-credit-lc} summarise the
validation curves.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{img/figure1_bank_learning_curve.png}
  \caption{Learning curve for Bank Marketing: validation PR-AUC versus number of
  training samples for XGBoost and MLP (five-fold stratified CV).}
  \label{fig:part2-bank-lc}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{img/figure2_credit_learning_curve.png}
  \caption{Learning curve for Credit Card Default: validation PR-AUC versus
  number of training samples for XGBoost and MLP (five-fold stratified CV).}
  \label{fig:part2-credit-lc}
\end{figure}

On Bank Marketing, both models improve as more training data become available,
but the trajectories differ. XGBoost already achieves a comparatively strong
validation PR-AUC with only a few thousand samples (approximately 0.57 at 3{,}616
examples) and continues to rise toward approximately 0.64 at the full training
size. The corresponding training PR-AUC falls as the sample grows, which is
expected: with little data the boosted trees overfit, and additional examples
regularise the fit while improving out-of-sample ranking. The MLP starts lower
and more unstable at small samples, then steadily approaches XGBoost without
fully closing the gap. This pattern indicates that marketing subscription
prediction benefits from additional labelled campaigns, but also that tree
ensembles extract useful structure earlier than the neural network under the
chosen architecture and training budget.

On Credit Card Default, absolute PR-AUC values remain lower than in the marketing
task, confirming Part~1's finding that default prediction is the harder problem.
XGBoost again improves monotonically with sample size (roughly 0.51 to 0.56),
while the MLP improves from a lower base and remains slightly behind at full
data. The persistent train--validation gap for XGBoost at small $n$, and its
gradual narrowing, suggest that further labelled defaults would still help, but
with diminishing returns: neither curve shows a sharp late jump that would imply
the models are data-starved at 24{,}000 examples. Practically, this supports
deploying the Part~1 models with the available data while recognising that credit
risk performance is bounded more by task difficulty and label noise than by
model choice alone.

Overall, the learning curves justify the Part~1 conclusion that XGBoost is the
stronger of the two methods under PR-AUC, and they show that this ranking is
stable across training-set sizes rather than an artefact of one data budget.

\subsection{Bias Analysis with Microsoft Fairlearn}
\label{sec:part2-fairness}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Observed bias was audited with Microsoft Fairlearn
\cite{bird2020fairlearn}.\footnote{Fairlearn documentation:
\url{https://fairlearn.org/}.}
Fairlearn was chosen because it integrates directly with scikit-learn-style
estimators, supports group-wise MetricFrame reporting, and provides standard
disparity measures (demographic parity difference and equalized odds difference)
that are widely used in responsible-ML reporting. Compared with dashboard-centric
tools such as Google What-If, Fairlearn is better suited to a scripted,
reproducible academic workflow that mirrors the Part~1 training pipeline.
IBM AIF360 would also have been viable; Fairlearn was preferred here for API
simplicity and for keeping the audit on the same XGBoost configuration used in
Part~1.

Two sensitive attributes were examined because they are salient in each domain:
age group for Bank Marketing (\texttt{<30}, \texttt{30--59}, \texttt{60+}) and
sex for Credit Card Default (male/female as coded in the UCI dataset). Age was
binned rather than used as a continuous variable so that disparity metrics could
be interpreted over policy-relevant cohorts. The audited model was the Part~1
XGBoost pipeline, trained on a stratified holdout and evaluated at a 0.5
decision threshold.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{img/figure3_bank_age_fairness.png}
  \caption{Fairlearn group metrics for Bank Marketing by age group: selection
  rate, true positive rate, and false positive rate.}
  \label{fig:part2-bank-fair}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{img/figure4_credit_sex_fairness.png}
  \caption{Fairlearn group metrics for Credit Card Default by sex: selection
  rate, true positive rate, and false positive rate.}
  \label{fig:part2-credit-fair}
\end{figure}

On Bank Marketing, disparities are large. The demographic parity difference is
approximately 0.42 and the equalized odds difference approximately 0.35.
Selection rates vary sharply by age: about 0.20 for ages 30--59, 0.34 for clients
under 30, and 0.62 for clients aged 60+. True positive rates are high across
groups (roughly 0.86--0.91), but the false positive rate for the 60+ group is
substantially elevated (approximately 0.48), which lowers group accuracy. In
marketing terms, older clients are predicted as subscribers far more often.
This may partly reflect genuine differences in historical conversion, but it
also means a deployed campaign optimiser could systematically over-contact older
customers and under-contact middle-aged customers. Because marketing contact is
itself an intervention, such disparities raise fairness concerns even when the
model maximises aggregate PR-AUC.

On Credit Card Default, sex disparities are smaller but not negligible.
Demographic parity and equalized odds differences are both near 0.057.
Males have a higher selection rate (approximately 0.32 versus 0.26 for females)
and a higher false positive rate (approximately 0.22 versus 0.17), while true
positive rates are similar (approximately 0.62 versus 0.60). In a lending or
collections context, even modest false-positive gaps can translate into unequal
scrutiny or adverse action. The smaller magnitude relative to the bank-age audit
does not imply fairness by default; it indicates that sex is a weaker axis of
disparity than age in the marketing model under the chosen threshold.

An important caveat is that fairness metrics depend on the decision threshold
and on whether the sensitive attribute is used as an input feature. Part~1
models included age (bank) and sex (credit) among predictors, which can amplify
disparate impact even when the feature is statistically predictive. A fuller
mitigation study could compare constrained optimisation or post-processing
reductions in Fairlearn; the present analysis is diagnostic rather than
remedial, which is appropriate for deliberation on the Part~1 methodology.

\subsection{Security, Privacy, and Ethical Risks at Scale}
\label{sec:part2-risks}
%----------------------------------------------------------

If the Part~1 methodology were deployed widely---for example as a bank-wide
campaign scorer or a credit early-warning system---several risks follow.

\paragraph{(i) Security.}
Model outputs and feature pipelines become attractive targets. An adversary who
can query a subscription or default score repeatedly may attempt membership
inference or model extraction, especially if probability scores are exposed
through an API. Feature-space attacks are also plausible: applicants or
intermediaries could manipulate reported income proxies, contact channels, or
repayment timing patterns to shift predicted risk. At large scale, pipeline
compromise (training-data poisoning or silent substitution of a challenger
model) could systematically mis-rank thousands of customers. Security controls
should therefore include access-controlled scoring endpoints, monitoring for
distributional drift and query abuse, signed model artefacts, and human review
for high-impact decisions.

\paragraph{(ii) Privacy.}
Both datasets contain attributes that are directly or indirectly identifying in
real deployments: age, job, marital status, education, sex, account behaviour,
and repayment history. Large-scale use implies retention of detailed behavioural
logs for retraining. That creates risks of function creep (marketing scores
reused for credit decisions), re-identification through quasi-identifiers, and
unauthorised secondary use. Privacy-preserving practice would require purpose
limitation, minimisation of sensitive fields where they are not necessary for
the decision, retention limits, encryption at rest, and---where regulation
demands it---rights to explanation and contestation. Training on historical
defaults also encodes past institutional decisions; without governance, those
histories are perpetuated under the appearance of objective automation.

\paragraph{(iii) Ethical risks.}
The Fairlearn results show that optimising Part~1's operational metrics does
not guarantee equitable treatment. A marketing model that over-selects older
clients can become manipulative if combined with aggressive sales practices; a
credit model with higher false positive rates for one sex can deepen unequal
access to financial opportunity. Deploying the same methodology in a different
application---for example, using repayment-style features for employment
screening or insurance pricing---would intensify ethical concern because the
original labels were collected for banking decisions, not for those new
purposes. Ethical deployment therefore requires explicit purpose constraints,
subgroup monitoring in production, threshold policies that reflect harm
asymmetry, and escalation to human decision-makers for adverse actions.
Transparency about uncertainty is also ethical practice: Part~1 already showed
that call duration inflates marketing performance through leakage; deploying
leaky features would create misleading confidence in targeting quality.

%----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Summary of the Deliberation}
\label{sec:part2-summary}
%----------------------------------------------------------

Part~2 supports three conclusions about the Part~1 analysis. First, stratified
holdout evaluation and stratified cross-validation provide a methodologically
sound basis for comparing XGBoost and MLP under imbalance, but they do not by
themselves make the models fair. Second, learning curves show that both models
benefit from more data, that XGBoost remains superior across training sizes, and
that credit default prediction is intrinsically harder than subscription
prediction. Third, Fairlearn reveals material age-related disparity in bank
marketing predictions and smaller but visible sex-related disparity in credit
default predictions. Any large-scale or cross-application deployment would
therefore need security hardening, privacy governance, and continuous fairness
monitoring in addition to the PR-AUC-oriented model selection used in Part~1.
